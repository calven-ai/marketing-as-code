# Forces, trend rules and ratings

Open this file at steps 3, 5, 6 and 7. It defines the five forces you sweep, the rule that routes each signal to exactly one of them, what a trend must be, and how to rate it.

## The five forces

| Force | What to surface | Keep | Reject (route elsewhere) | Evidence bar |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Macro | The economic and market structure climate for the category: buyer budgets and software spend, the funding climate, vendor M&A and consolidation, layoffs, pricing model shifts | Budget and spend surveys, funding climate, consolidation, a competitor's funding or acquisition when it shows the climate | Product launches (technology), laws (regulatory), sanctions and sovereignty (geopolitical) | One solid source acceptable, stated as lower confidence |
| Technology | Shifts reshaping the category: AI and agents, new architectures, capabilities becoming table stakes, competitor product launches that show where the technology is heading | Capability shifts, launches that move the bar for everyone | Funding and M&A (macro), laws (regulatory), sanctions (geopolitical) | Two or more independent sources |
| Regulatory | Laws, regulation, compliance standards and rulings that gate how the category's vendors or buyers sell and operate (AI regulation, privacy law, data residency law, audit and security standards) | Only actual laws, standards and rulings that gate deals | Launches, funding, M&A, generic tech news | Two or more independent sources |
| Geopolitical | Events with market impact: sanctions, export controls, trade actions, data sovereignty by region, regional market access | Only those | Launches, funding, M&A, generic regulation | Two or more independent sources |
| Category shift | Structural change to the category or the competitive map itself: a credible new entrant or AI native challenger, a consolidation wave, a category created, collapsing or renamed by analysts or press, a sub-category splitting off, an adjacent platform absorbing the category's job, the pricing or business model norm moving (usage pricing becoming default, product-led displacing sales-led, a credible open source alternative), the buyer or budget moving to another function, the capital climate turning | Only material structural shifts; high bar, lots of noise | One tracked competitor's own release, price change, repositioning, funding or acquisition (that is a competitor move); broad funding climate (macro) | One solid source acceptable, stated as lower confidence |

Rule of thumb for the last row: one competitor changing its price is a competitor move; the category's pricing norm moving is a category shift. An acquisition of or by a tracked competitor is a competitor move; a consolidation wave, or an acquirer from outside the space entering it, is a category shift.

## What every trend must be

- **Titled:** a headline of 3 to 7 words in Title Case.
- **Described** in markdown built for scanning: a 1 to 2 sentence opening that names the trend, then 3 to 4 one-line bullets with the key dynamics and evidence (name the source where it carries weight), then one closing line on why it matters to the buyer. No headings. About 120 words in total; structure replaces prose, it must not add length. Make it rich enough that someone could build a market opportunity on it.
- **About the market, competitors, buyers or regulation:** never about the company itself, never generic tech news.
- **Sourced** from reputable outlets.
- **Actionable for the company:** a so what written from the company's seat. A short paragraph (1 to 2 short sentences) with the concrete implication, naming the segment, message, motion or deal dynamic of theirs it touches; then 2 to 3 one-line bullets only if there is more than one distinct point; then a final paragraph starting `**Consider:**` with the single move (a message, a segment, or a competitor to counter: pick one). About 80 words. Lead with the implication itself, never filler like "this is important because", never a restatement of the description.
- **Rated, fresh and dated, non-redundant** (one trend per dynamic; extend an existing one instead of repeating it).

## Severity: the consequence for the company, not the size of the topic

Severity answers "what does this force the company to change?", not "how important is this in the market?". A trend can be major for the industry and still be Medium for this company.

| Level | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| High | Changes what the company does this quarter: a plan it would rewrite, a segment it would re-prioritize, a message or price it would change now |
| Medium | It acts on it, on the normal planning cycle. Most trends belong here |
| Low | Real, well-sourced context worth watching, with nothing to act on yet. A genuine destination, not a level to promote out of |

High is the exception: expect at most one per report. If two candidates look High, keep the one that forces a decision sooner and make the other Medium. When torn between two levels, pick the lower one. Re-rate on every refresh; a trend that was High last time is not High by default.

## Horizon

| Horizon | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Now | Already affecting deals, budgets or messages |
| 6 to 12 months | Visible and building; will bite within the year |
| 12+ months | Directional; worth tracking, not yet in deals |
