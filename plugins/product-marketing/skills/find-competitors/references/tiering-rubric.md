# Tiering rubric

Open this at step 5 (emerging bar), step 6 (gates), step 7 (ranking) and step 8 (naming).

## What counts as a competitor

A competitor sells a similar product to a similar buyer: something a prospect would seriously evaluate instead of the company for the same job.

- **Same category and same buyer.** Required for Tier 1 and Tier 2. A tool in an adjacent category (observability when the company is a data platform, A/B testing when it is a recommender, an infrastructure layer when it is a full platform) is Tier 3 at most.
- **Independent and distinct.** Never list the company's own spin-offs, subsidiaries or sibling brands. A name that appears only on "alternatives" SEO pages but sells something else, or is a related entity, is a false positive: verify the category and that it is a separate company.
- **A purchasable finished product.** A developer framework, library or cloud building-block API is usually not a rival to a finished product at all. Leave it off.

## The shortlist test: demote before you drop

Could a real prospect put this on the same shortlist as the company, even rarely? Yes: tier it, at Tier 3 when the overlap is only at the edges. No: drop it. A vendor that merely co-appears on broad lists ("AI tools", "data tools") is not automatically a competitor; one a prospect would genuinely weigh against the company is, however rarely.

Drop only on an existence failure, never on a priority one:

- not an independent company (spin-off, subsidiary, sibling brand);
- a framework, library or building-block API rather than a product;
- no website of its own (a category or a feature, not a company);
- discontinued, or absorbed so fully that nothing is sold under the name.

## Alive and independent today

Roundups go stale. A name you saw only in roundups and never on its own live site this run is a red flag: read its homepage directly to confirm it is still sold, or drop it. Use news only to check whether a competitor was recently acquired, merged or discontinued, which should change its tier.

What counts as a change of identity: it now sells under another name, it became part of another company, or it closed. A funding round, private-equity ownership, or a deal where it bought someone else and kept its name does not count.

## Ranking evidence, strongest first

Tiering is a ranking, not a pass/fail bar. Order candidates by closeness on public evidence:

1. **Same category and same buyer:** a prospect solving the same job with the same budget owner would evaluate both.
2. **Direct comparison:** the company's own "vs" or "alternatives" pages name them; they publish "<company> alternatives" or "<them> vs <company>" pages; review sites list them as alternatives; category roundups list both side by side.
3. **Same segment and motion:** similar customer size, buying motion (self-serve or sales-led) and region.

Momentum and analyst standing break ties within this order and never override the same-category, same-buyer test.

## Tiers

- **Tier 1, head-to-head.** Two or three rivals, never more than four: same category, same buyer, strongest comparison evidence. A full run always has a Tier 1; when evidence is thin, the closest same-category, same-buyer rivals are still Tier 1, because the tier is relative. The category's default vendor that buyers compare against is Tier 1 even when far larger; a rival the company's own size in its exact niche is Tier 1 when it sells to the same buyer. If the company sells several products, Tier 1 is still one ranking across all of them; a product whose closest rival misses the top four gets that rival at Tier 2.
- **Tier 2, category competitors.** Four to six. Same category, with a somewhat different segment, size band, region or use case, or weaker comparison evidence than Tier 1.
- **Tier 3, adjacent and edge.** Two or three. Tangential players, vendors aimed at a different part of the market, companies that overlap only at the edges. A real destination, not a leftovers bin.

Popularity never earns Tier 1. A big brand in a neighbouring category, a platform the company integrates with or builds on, a broad suite where one module overlaps, or a vendor whose buyer is a different team stays at Tier 2 or 3 however often it shows up. For a mid-market specialist, a large enterprise suite that covers the job as one module among many is the classic Tier 3 case: a real competitor to track.

A single named company with no stated tier: Tier 1 only when it meets the company head-to-head (same category, buyer, size band and buying motion); Tier 2 for a real category competitor in a different segment or use case; Tier 3 for an edge player. When torn, the lower tier.

## Emerging bar

An emerging entrant passes all three checks.

1. **Same buyer, same job.** From its own site, you can finish this sentence honestly: "<Entrant> sells <job> to <buyer>, which is what <company> sells to <buyer>." A different job (consumer photo editing against enterprise asset management) or a different buyer (hobbyists or individual developers against an enterprise team) is off-market, however well its keywords matched. Sharing a broad umbrella ("AI", "automation", "data") is not overlap.
2. **Actually new.** Founded within about five years, or latest disclosed round seed to Series B, or first product in the company's category launched within about two years. Series C and later, public, acquired by an incumbent, or a decade-old vendor is established: tier it instead.
3. **Credible.** A real product and a real launch, funding or traction signal in a source you read. Not vaporware, not a one-line mention.

Evidence: check 1 needs the entrant's own site, read this run; a news article tells you it exists, not what it sells or to whom. Checks 2 and 3 may rest on any page you read (a funding announcement, a launch article, a company profile). Do not search again only to reconfirm a funding stage an article already showed.

Label each entrant **Direct** (same core job and same buyer) or **Adjacent** (a neighbouring product the same team could buy instead of part of what the company sells, for example a nearby category now shipping the company's job as a usable feature). A company that only integrates with, supplies, or serves the same team for a different task is off-market.

On a list that already captures the category's credible newcomers, raise the bar: a new name earns a row only as a standout the list is clearly missing (meaningful funding, real traction, or a genuinely new sub-category). Two solid entrants beat six padded ones.

## Naming

- The brand the website sells under, as the site writes it. A site titled "Relay, a Northstar company" is "Relay", never "Northstar".
- An acquisition does not rename the row: a site that now redirects to its acquirer keeps its pre-deal brand, and the deal goes in the description. The acquirer is a different row.
- A rename does: the same company under a new name, with no buyer or merger partner, takes the new name.
- The brand, not a plan or edition ("Relay Enterprise" is "Relay"). No legal suffix, no tagline.
- Size describes the company that sells it today: a startup inside a large vendor is not a startup.
