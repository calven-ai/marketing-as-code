# Messaging frameworks and quality tests

Condensed from the brand messaging and positioning skill in the GTM plugin.
The frameworks below are the ones `strategy/messaging.md` is shaped around;
the tests at the end are the gate before a diff is proposed.

## Four message layers, in order

1. Clarity: say what you are, category first, before any cleverness.
2. Relevance: show it is for them, naming their specific pain.
3. Value: the concrete benefit, quantified where a snapshot allows.
4. Differentiation: why you, and not the alternative.

Clear messaging about a plain product beats brilliant messaging about an
unclear one. Do not write layer four before layers one to three hold.

## The messaging house

- Roof: the one core promise (the positioning statement's benefit, in one
  sentence).
- Three pillars: the primary themes, each a distinct customer concern with
  two or three proof points.
- Foundation: purpose, vision and values that ground the pillars.

Every line in every channel should trace up to a pillar and to the roof.

## The positioning statement (Moore)

For [target customer] who [need], [product] is a [category] that [key
benefit]. Unlike [competitive alternative], it [primary differentiation].
Naming the alternative is not optional.

## Dunford's five components, in dependency order

Competitive alternatives → unique attributes → value → target customer →
market category. `strategy/positioning.md` already follows this order; the
messaging house consumes its output.

## Copy primitives to derive from the house

One-liner ([problem] + [solution] + [result]); a 30 to 60 second elevator
pitch; three to five tagline options; hero headline and subhead; channel
variants for social and ads. Each is derived, never written from scratch.

## Quality tests before the diff

| Test | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Appeal | Does the target customer actually want this benefit (research, not assumption)? |
| Exclusivity | Could only we credibly say it? Check against competitor copy. |
| Clarity | Understood inside five seconds, no jargon? |
| Credibility | Is there proof: a number, a named customer, a quote? |

Story diagnostic, seven yes or no questions: a stranger understands the
offer in five seconds; the customer is the hero, the brand the guide; the
problem is named at the external, internal and philosophical level; the
guide shows empathy and authority; there is an obvious three-step plan;
one call to action; the cost of doing nothing and the picture of success
are both stated.

## Mistakes to catch in review

| Mistake | Fix |
| --- | --- |
| Features instead of benefits | Translate: "lead scoring" becomes "spend time only on leads three times likelier to buy" |
| Vague language | Would a stranger understand it? |
| Generic differentiation | If a competitor can say it, it is not a pillar |
| Unsubstantiated claims | "Best in class" needs a snapshot or a customer behind it |
| Brand as hero | Reposition the brand as the guide |
| No call to action | Say what the reader does next |

## Principles worth keeping

Customer understanding precedes creative work; clarity before cleverness;
consistency compounds; proof beats assertion; messaging is a hypothesis
until buyers have heard it.
