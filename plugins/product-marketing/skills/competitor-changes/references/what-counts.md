# What counts as a fact, page by page

A competitor's site is full of words and light on facts. For each kind of page, this file says what to report and what to ignore. Report what is there; deciding what matters comes later.

## Announcement or content?

For a newly published page (a blog post, a newsroom item, a new landing page), ask one question: does this page state a new first-party fact about the company that published it, something that became true and was not true before? Content explains, teaches, argues, predicts and comments, and can be excellent while containing no fact about the publisher. "We acquired a company" is an announcement; an essay on the future of the market is content.

Format never decides. An event page can carry an analyst placement next to the booth number, a culture post can end with an executive change, a monthly update can bury a pricing change in paragraph nine. Read for the fact; do not classify by page type.

For an edited page, the edit is the news. Ignore navigation, cookie text, copyright years, reordered sections, image names, whitespace, and rotating testimonials or statistics. A page whose only changes are churn has nothing to report, and that is common.

## Pricing

The most deliberate page a company publishes; report every change to what a buyer pays or gets.

- A price that moved, appeared or disappeared.
- A plan or tier added, removed, renamed, merged or split.
- What a tier includes: seats, usage limits, quotas, overage rates, entitlements moving between tiers.
- Free tier or trial: length, existence, card requirement, limits.
- Billing terms: annual discount, minimum commitment, contract length, payment terms.
- A "contact sales" flip in either direction. That is a packaging decision, not layout.
- Add-ons and their prices.
- A new pricing unit (per seat becoming per usage, per credit, per workflow). The unit changing matters more than the number.

Watch the unit, not just the number: "$50 per user per month" becoming "$50 per user per month, billed annually" is a real change, and so is "unlimited" becoming a number. Ignore logo rotation, FAQ rewording that changes no term, and reordered comparison rows.

## Positioning

How the company describes itself. The noisiest kind of edited page.

- A category claim: the market it places itself in. A one-word category change ("metrics observability" to "data observability") looks trivial and is the most valuable thing to catch; quote both sides.
- The stated audience: a segment, industry, role or company size added, dropped or widened.
- A named product, platform or tier introduced, renamed or retired.
- A value claim that carries a fact: a number, a guarantee, a named outcome.
- A competitor named, a comparison introduced, a migration offer.
- Proof that carries a number or a name: a customer logo, a metric, an analyst placement, a certification.

Not a change: the same claim reworded (if describing it needs "reworded", "refreshed", "tightened" or "clarified", it is not a fact), sweeping prose with no fact under it ("reimagines", "redefines", "elevates"), sections moving, hero image swaps, a rotating statistic ticking up.

## Product motion

Changelogs, release notes, what's-new pages, roadmaps. Almost every entry is a change; the question is which ones name a capability.

- A new product or module.
- A major capability inside an existing product, especially one their seller would demo.
- A capability reaching general availability, leaving beta, or entering early access.
- A capability retired, deprecated or sunset: as strong as a launch and easier to miss.
- A platform or deployment change: new region, cloud, runtime, self-hosted option.
- A named third-party integration.
- Anything on a roadmap page: a statement of intent.
- A limit or entitlement stated here before it reaches the pricing page.

Not a fact: bug fixes, performance tweaks, dependency bumps, improvements with no named capability. A version number is context, not the fact; report what is in the version.

## Trust and security

Security pages, trust centers, compliance pages, subprocessor lists. Every word is signed off, so a real edit is presumed reportable.

- A certification or attestation gained or lost (SOC 2 Type I or II, ISO 27001, ISO 42001, HIPAA, PCI DSS, FedRAMP and its level, and regional schemes). Record the period it covers: a renewal and a first attestation read alike otherwise.
- A subprocessor added or removed, by name. An AI vendor appearing on a subprocessor list often precedes an AI feature by a quarter; it looks like housekeeping and is one of the most valuable finds.
- A new data region or residency option.
- A customer-facing security capability: SSO, SCIM, audit logs, customer-managed keys, private networking, IP allowlisting.
- A compliance claim about a framework or regulation.
- A penetration test, audit cadence or bug bounty appearing or changing.

Ignore status-page incident history, a reworded security philosophy, badge image swaps, contact changes.

## Legal

Privacy policy, terms of service, DPA, acceptable use. Lawyers edit these because the business changed, and policy moves before the product does.

- A new category of data collected or processed (voice recordings, screen contents, model inputs): the strongest tell.
- A new processing purpose, especially model training, personalisation or analytics.
- A new subprocessor or recipient.
- A new plan, tier or product name in the terms; packaging often lands here before the pricing page.
- A new contracting entity, jurisdiction or governing law: a market entry, stated drily.
- A new data-transfer mechanism.
- Retention periods changing.
- Liability, indemnity or SLA terms changing, or a new enterprise-only clause.
- An AI or automated-decision clause appearing.

Ignore consent-banner churn, reformatted contents, address changes, a "last updated" date with no substantive edit. These documents are long and mostly renumbering: find the one clause whose meaning moved and quote it. No such sentence, nothing to report.

## Press

Newsroom items usually carry a fact; the work is extraction. One release often holds several facts (amount, valuation, lead investor, use of funds); record each, then treat them as one event.

- Funding: amount, round, valuation, investors, total raised.
- Acquisition: who bought whom, price if stated, what happens to the team.
- Executive change: role, name, predecessor, where they came from.
- Launch, partnership (named partner and joint offering), customer milestone (a named customer or a counted one), financial milestone (ARR, growth, profitability), certification, expansion (country, office, region, segment), recognition (an analyst placement or an award from a named body).

Not a release: an index page, an event promotion, a media kit, a syndicated third-party article, an award shortlist the company entered itself. The dateline is not the fact. For a recap of an earlier announcement, date the fact by the original announcement.

## Market activity

Blogs, customer stories, integration pages, events, resource libraries. About nine in ten items are content and yield nothing. The exceptions:

- A named customer. "A leading global bank" is content; a named bank is a customer win.
- A named integration.
- A launch announced on the blog rather than the newsroom.
- A pricing or packaging change explained in a post.
- An explicit repositioning: a post whose subject is that the company now defines itself differently.
- A competitor named: a comparison, a migration guide, a "why we are different from X".
- A first-party number about the company: customers, ARR, usage, growth.

Everything else (how-tos, opinion, predictions, explainers, listicles, recaps, webinars, podcasts, ebooks, culture posts) is content.

## Not a change at all

Many sites adapt pages to the visitor's location. A different currency symbol or the same price converted, different number or date formatting, the same text in another language or spelling, a region-specific banner: none of these is a change. A switched currency whose numbers no conversion explains ($12 becoming £15) is a regional price list, recorded beside the existing figures and labelled by currency, not a price change. Zero is zero in every currency.
