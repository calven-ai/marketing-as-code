# Which product to profile

Open this when the company may sell more than one thing (step 3), when the user asks to profile one named product, or when writing a portfolio overview.

## The two tests

A product is something the company sells on its own, to its own audience. Both must pass:

1. **It can be bought by itself.** A customer could buy this and nothing else and get something usable.
2. **It has its own audience.** The people who evaluate and use it are a distinguishable set, not simply more of the same buyer.

Never products, however prominently marketed:

- **Tiers, plans and editions** (Free, Pro, Business, Enterprise; Standard versus Premium): the same product, more or less of it.
- **Modules, add-ons and extensions** that only work on top of something else. If it cannot be bought without the parent, it is part of the parent.
- **Features** with a marketing name and a page of their own. A named feature is still a feature.
- **Solution and use-case pages** ("for Marketing", "for Healthcare", "for Startups"): the same product aimed at a segment.
- **Services:** onboarding, support plans, professional services, training.
- **The company itself**, or its umbrella brand, when everything it sells is one product under one name.

When you cannot tell whether something is a separate product or a tier of another, it is not a separate product. Under-listing costs the user one correction; over-listing means a profile, positioning and messaging get built for a product that does not exist.

## One product or several

Most companies sell one product. That is the normal answer, not a failed search.

1. **Read the pricing page first.** What a customer can buy separately appears as a separate line; what is really a tier appears as a column of the same table.
2. **Read the top navigation.** A company with several products almost always says so in its main menu. A single-product company's menu is features, solutions and resources.
3. **Classify every candidate** against the two tests, strictly.
4. **Report the verdict** (single product or several), the products, the pages it rests on, and every rejected candidate with the test it failed.

When the user says the company sells several products, that settles whether; your job becomes which. Prefer the divisions the company itself names (its modules or suites usually are the products then), and split where the buyer changes: two capabilities bought by the same person on the same contract are one product, two bought by different teams are two. Still refuse tiers, deployment options, single features, industry pages and services. If you can honestly justify only one, say so and name what you considered; never invent a second.

## Profiling one named product

Profile exactly the product the user named, nothing else, even as you pass the company's other products, tiers and integrations. Start from the company's own page for it, then pricing if the product page is thin. If the company's own site says nothing about the name, say so rather than filling the profile from a guess: a wrong description is worse than a missing one. If the name is a tier, module, add-on, feature or use-case page, do not profile it as a product: say what it is and which product it belongs to. The user can still overrule you.

## Portfolio overview

For a multi-product company the company-level overview is separate from each product's profile. Write it after the product profiles exist, from them for product facts and from the company's own pages for company facts.

- **Portfolio Overview:** the company as its own pages present it: one-liner, history (founding, mergers, acquisitions), scale and footprint (customers, users, countries), named customers and segments, recognition.
- **Products & How They Fit Together:** one row for every product: what it does, who buys it, and the data, workflow or platform link to the rest. A product you cannot describe is a row saying so, never an omitted row. Keep it thin; each product's profile carries the depth.
- **Expansion & Cross-Sell Paths** and **Cross-Product Pricing & Bundling:** only what the site states about combinations: a published bundle, a suite price, a stated "start here, add that" path. A cross-sell path you reasoned out from the product list is an inference at best: label it or leave the gap.
- **Shared Platform & Architecture:** only what is genuinely common: the identity layer, the data model, the deployment and security posture every product inherits. A capability one product has belongs in that product's profile. If the site presents no shared platform, say so plainly; an invented platform story is the most damaging thing this section can hold, because everything downstream treats it as technical ground truth.
- **Portfolio Differentiators:** what the whole portfolio wins on that no single product does alone. If you cannot name what the combination adds, say so rather than restating the strongest product's pitch.
