# Speaker rules

Open this when a transcript labels turns with anything other than a clear name and side.

## Sides

- **Vendor side:** anyone speaking for the person's company: sales reps, solutions engineers, customer success, founders demoing the product.
- **Customer side:** anyone on the buying or using side: buyers, users, evaluators, practitioners being interviewed.
- **Unknown:** when you are not confident. Never guess.

When the attendee list does not say which side someone is on, work it out from the dialogue: who demos and answers product questions is the vendor side; who asks about fit, price and their own situation is the customer side. Someone describing "our customer" or "our product" in your company's terms is on the vendor side whatever the label says.

## Only name people from the attendee list

- Pick a person only from the attendee list provided. Never infer a name from a first name mentioned inside the dialogue, and never assign a name you are not sure of.
- One person, one label: never give the same attendee to two labels that both spoke.
- When no attendee list exists, name nobody; judge only the side.

## Labels that are not people

- **Anonymous labels** (`Speaker A`, `Speaker 1`): resolve from the attendee list and the dialogue, or leave the individual unknown and give the side.
- **Local channel labels** (`microphone`, `mic`, `me`): whoever ran the recorder, which on a sales call is your own rep. Never the customer.
- **Output channel labels** (`speaker`, `system`, `them`): every remote participant mixed onto one track. On a four-person call it is three people. Give the side, not a person.
- **Side-only heads** (`Customer side`, `Our side`): the side is certain, the person usually is not. Name the speaker only when the attendee list puts exactly one person on that side and nothing in the dialogue shows anyone else speaking for it (a second name addressed, "my colleague here").

A wrong name attributes someone's words to a real person. Leaving the individual unknown is the correct answer whenever you are not certain.

## Advisors and practitioner interviews

A practitioner who is not buying (a research interview, an advisor, a peer) is customer side when talking about their own work, their tools and their reaction to your product. Their advice to you (which segment to pick, how to price) is not buyer evidence; quote it only when it carries a concrete market fact from their own experience, and label the role Practitioner.
