# Positioning

## Positioning Statement

> **For** support and operations leads at mid-market SaaS companies
> **who** lose customer trust every time an outage is explained late, in
> the wrong place, or not at all, **Beacon** is **the incident communication
> platform** **that** turns one update into the right message on the status
> page, in the product, and in the inbox, at once. **Unlike** the status
> page bundled with a monitoring suite, **Beacon** is written for the person
> who has to talk to customers, not the person who has to fix the server.

| Component | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Target customer | Head of Support or Head of Operations at a SaaS company with 50 to 500 employees |
| Need / opportunity | Explain incidents to customers fast enough, and consistently enough, that trust survives the outage |
| Product name | Beacon |
| Market category | Incident communication |
| Key benefit | One update reaches every customer channel in under a minute, in the company's voice |
| Primary alternative | The status page that comes bundled with the monitoring suite, plus a support agent writing emails by hand |
| Differentiation | Built for the communicator: templates in the company's voice, audience segments, and a post-incident report the customer actually reads |

## Market Category & Frame of Reference

- **Category we claim:** incident communication. A deliberate reframe: the
  buyer's mental model is "status page", which is a feature of monitoring
  tools; we sell the whole conversation with the customer.
- **Analyst frame of reference:** none yet. Analysts file status pages under
  IT service management.
- **Why this frame:** "status page" undersells the product to a support
  leader, who does not own monitoring and would never buy from that budget.
  "Incident communication" names their job.

## Competitive Alternatives

| Alternative | What it is | Why a buyer considers it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Northstar | The status page inside a monitoring suite | Already paid for; engineering set it up years ago |
| Doing it by hand | A support agent writing emails and a tweet during the incident | It worked last time, mostly |
| A generic email tool | Sending an outage email to the whole list | Cheap, and every company already has one |

## Unique Attributes

- **Voice templates:** every update starts from a template written in the
  company's voice, reviewed once, reused every time.
- **Audience segments:** affected customers get the update; unaffected ones
  do not, so the update is a courtesy instead of an alarm.
- **The customer-facing post-incident report:** a plain-language summary a
  customer success manager can send the next morning without editing.

| Audience segments | Fewer tickets from customers who were never affected | Support team leads |
| Customer-facing report | Renewal conversations stop starting with "about that outage" | Customer success |

## Best-Fit Customer Characteristics

- SaaS companies whose product is in the customer's daily workflow, so an
  outage is felt within minutes
- 50 to 500 employees: a support team of at least five, and a budget owner
  for its tools
- A monitoring suite already in place, so the status page exists and is
  visibly not enough
- A recent incident that generated a ticket spike or an escalation from a
  large account
- Customers in more than one region or plan tier, so one message for all of
  them is wrong for most

## Relevant Market Trends (why now)

- **Customers expect a message before they notice:** the standard for
  incident updates is set by the largest platforms, and every SaaS company
  is measured against it.
- **Support owns retention:** support and success teams are now judged on
  renewals, and an outage handled badly is the cheapest way to lose one.
- **Monitoring suites keep bundling:** the bundled status page is good
  enough for engineering and worse every year for the customer, which
  opens the gap we sell into.
