# What customers want during an outage

Nobody wants a status page. They want to know three things, in this order:
that you know, whether it affects them, and what happens next. A status
page answers the first one, for the customers who think to look. The other
two are the whole job, and they are the ones that decide whether the
customer is still a customer next year.

## They want to hear it from you first

The worst sentence in a renewal call is "I found out from a customer." Your
monitoring caught the incident at 09:14. Your customers noticed at 09:15.
The question is who told them, and when. A first update inside a minute
does not need to explain anything. It needs to exist: we know, we are on
it, more in fifteen minutes. The teams that manage that are not faster
writers. They have the first update written already, in their own voice,
waiting for the incident.

## They want to know whether it is about them

An incident in one region, on one plan, for one feature, and every customer
gets the alarm. Half the tickets that arrive during an outage come from
people who were never affected and now think they are. That is not a
communication problem; it is an audience problem. Tell the customers who
are affected. Leave the others alone. Their afternoon was fine until you
told them it was not.

## They want something to forward

The next morning, your customer's boss asks what happened. Your customer
needs a page to send: what broke, who it affected, what changed so it will
not happen again, in language a boss can read. The engineering postmortem
is not that page. The customer-facing report is, and it is the last thing
the customer remembers about the incident. Make it the best-written thing
you send all year.
