# Quote rules

Open this before cutting quotes (step 5) and again before rating them (step 7).

## The passage rule

A quote is a passage you found, not a sentence you wrote. Start from one unbroken stretch of the transcript and tidy it in place. Every word you keep is a word that is there, in the order it is there.

- **Do:** fix spelling, grammar, tense and agreement; fix capitalization and punctuation; remove filler and disfluencies (um, you know, like, I mean, sort of, false starts); spell company and competitor names in full.
- **Do not:** add a word, clause or parenthetical; reorder what was said; summarize the ending in your own words; join two stretches that are not next to each other.

Example: `Yeah so um the Northstar page it was kind of a pain honestly` becomes `The Northstar page was a pain.`

**The ending is where this goes wrong.** A trailing clause you rephrase makes the quote impossible to find in the call.

- Passage: *"we kept Pagewise because, I mean, it was cheap, it was like thirty bucks, but then nobody, the support team, nobody could send anything to just the EU customers"*
  - Wrong: `We kept Pagewise because it was cheap, but it could not target EU customers.` (the ending is yours)
  - Right: `We kept Pagewise because it was cheap. But then the support team could not send anything to just the EU customers.`
- Passage: *"Northstar's fine, honestly, for the engineers, they love it, it's the email part that's, that's us writing it by hand at 2am"*
  - Wrong: `Northstar is fine for engineers (but support writes emails manually).` (the parenthetical is yours)
  - Right: `Northstar's fine for the engineers. It's the email part that's us writing it by hand at 2am.`

On a machine transcript of live speech, the best quote is usually the shortest unbroken stretch, often one or two sentences, that carries the point once the filler is gone. Two good sentences with other talk between them are two quotes. Keep the speaker's own words even when a native speaker would choose others; swapping a word is rewriting. If no stretch reads cleanly after removing filler, skip it.

## Self-contained, one idea

- Each quote is one claim that stands on its own. Prefer a stretch that names its own subject. When the subject was named earlier, never add it to the quote: say what "it", "that" or "they" refers to in "Why it was said".
- A bare "Yes" or "Exactly" is never a quote. A short answer is one only when the question goes in "Why it was said".
- Split a multi-part statement; never staple unrelated points together.
- One quote per point: when the same point comes up again later, quote the clearest, most specific time.
- Keep each quote under about 100 words; past that, take the strongest unbroken part.

## Vendor-side quotes need a claim

A rep quote earns a row when a messaging lead could quote it back as "this is what we tell buyers about <rival>": one claim, one subject. Skip the rep restating the buyer, thinking out loud, and feature narration with no claim.

## Rating

- **Critical:** the standout evidence a marketer would build a slide on: a rival named with the reason they are in or out, a quantified cost or result against a rival, a decisive competitive objection, a sharp differentiation line from your rep, or a line so well put it would be quoted word for word.
- **High:** clearly useful and specific to this call: a concrete complaint or strength of a rival, a detail only this buyer would give, a real concession or pricing comparison.
- **Medium:** relevant and accurate but generic: what many buyers say in the same words.
- **Low:** not worth a row: agreement, restating the other side, a passing name with no content, a fragment, or a line that only makes sense next to the dialogue around it.

A quote that still reads like raw transcription is at most medium however good its point. Same topic is not same point: a cost and its cause, or two stakeholders' positions, are distinct evidence.
